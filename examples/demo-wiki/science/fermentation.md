# Fermentation

**Summary**: Microbes convert sugars into gas, acids and aroma while the dough rests. It is the slowest step in baking, and the one that decides most of the flavour.

**Sources**: starter-log.md, bake-tests.md

Fermentation does three things at once: it produces gas, develops flavour and changes dough strength. A baker controls it with only two levers, time and temperature, so most recipes are really a schedule.

## Gas production

[[Yeast]] feeds on simple sugars and gives off carbon dioxide and ethanol. The gas is trapped by the [[gluten]] network, which stretches around each bubble instead of letting it escape (Source: bake-tests.md).

### Where the sugar comes from

Flour holds very little free sugar. Enzymes in the flour break damaged starch down into sugars the yeast can use, which is why a long rest keeps producing gas long after the first food has run out. [[Starch-gelatinisation]] later finishes the job in the oven.

### Temperature

Yeast works faster as dough warms, roughly doubling its pace with every ten degrees until it starts to suffer. Warm dough rises quickly and tastes of little. Cool dough rises slowly and has time to develop.

> **Note:** A dough that has doubled in an hour and one that has doubled overnight look alike, and bake into very different loaves.

## Acid production

Lactic acid bacteria dominate in a [[sourdough-starter]] and give a [[sourdough-loaf]] its tang. They also matter a great deal in [[rye-bread]], where acidity tames enzymes that would otherwise turn the crumb gummy (Source: starter-log.md).

Two acids do most of the work, and a baker can shift the balance between them.

| Acid | Tastes | Favoured by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lactic | Mild, like yoghurt | Wet, warm starters |
| Acetic | Sharp, like vinegar | Firm, cool starters |

Acid also changes the dough itself. It tightens the gluten at first, then slowly weakens it, so a very sour dough can be strong in the morning and slack by evening (Source: starter-log.md; bake-tests.md).

## Bulk and final

The first rise builds flavour and strength; folds such as [[stretch-and-fold]] happen during this stage. The second is [[proofing]], which is about the shape of the loaf and the last of its gas.

### Reading the dough

Volume says more than the clock. Look for a dough that has grown by half to double, feels airy, and shows bubbles along the sides of the container.

> [!warning] Overproofing
> A dough left too long loses its structure. It spreads in the oven instead of rising, and no amount of [[scoring]] can save it.

### Going cold

Long cold fermentation is described in [[retarding-dough]], which has not been written yet. In short, the fridge slows yeast far more than it slows bacteria, so the dough gains flavour without running out of strength.

## What ends it

Fermentation stops in the oven, at around 60 °C, when the yeast dies. The last burst of gas before that is the oven spring, and everything after belongs to the [[maillard-reaction]].
